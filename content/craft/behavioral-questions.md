# Behavioral Questions

Section V of *Cracking the Coding Interview*: the soft questions are the most coachable part of an interview, and the candidates who ace them prepared a grid.

> **Note:** This module is a study guide written for Atlas CE, not a copy. Cracking the Coding Interview is a commercial book by Gayle Laakmann McDowell — buy it at [careercup.com](https://www.careercup.com/book). Each topic below distills one idea from the book's fifth section.

## Build the Preparation Grid

Rows: **challenges, mistakes and failures, what you enjoyed, leadership, conflicts, what you'd do differently.** Columns: every major item on your resume — each project, job, and activity. Each cell holds a story reduced to a couple of keywords, enough to study from and to glance at mentally in an interview.

Filling the grid converts "tell me about a time…" from recall under pressure into retrieval of prepared material. Alongside it, know 1–3 projects in deep technical detail where you played the central role — you'll be asked to drill into them.

**The norm:** every resume line × every question type = a story you can tell in two sentences.

## Nugget First, Then Situation–Action–Result

Structure every behavioral answer the same way: open with the **nugget** — a one-line summary of the story's point ("that project taught me to escalate early — here's an example") — then give **SAR: Situation, Action, Result**, keeping situation and result succinct because the **action is the important part**. Break the action into numbered steps: "I did three things — first…". The interviewer can stop you anywhere and still have your point.

Answering rules: be specific, not arrogant — give facts and let the interviewer draw conclusions; limit detail — state the key points and offer to drill in; and focus on **yourself, not the team** (interviews assess individuals — watch your "we" to "I" ratio).

**The norm:** headline first, then situation briefly, action in steps, result in numbers.

## Weaknesses and Questions to Ask

For weaknesses, give a **real, legitimate weakness plus how you work around it** — the double positive: "I don't have great attention to detail, so I always have someone double-check my work." "I work too hard" reads as arrogance and answers nothing.

Prepare three kinds of questions to ask your interviewer: **genuine** (team ratios, what brought you here), **insightful** (show you researched their tech choices), and **passion** (learning opportunities, curiosity about their stack). Asking nothing is the only wrong answer.

**The norm:** a real weakness with a real mitigation; three prepared questions minimum.

## Know What Each Story Says About You

Every story signals a trait: initiative, leadership, empathy, humility, teamwork. Before the interview, audit each grid cell and ask what it says about you — if the honest answer is "nothing", rework the telling or pick a different story. "The client wanted a call, so I called him myself because I knew he'd appreciate hearing it directly from me" turns a service ticket into evidence of ownership.

**The norm:** a story without a trait is an anecdote; choose stories that testify.

## "Tell Me About Yourself"

The pitch runs roughly chronologically and works for ~95% of candidates: current role (headline only) → college → the path since → details of current role → what you do outside work → wrap with **why this company**. Condense early history as you gain experience. Mention hobbies only if they're unique, technical, or demonstrate a trait, and sprinkle evidence of success (recruited, launched, awarded) where it fits naturally.

**The norm:** sixty to ninety seconds, chronological, ending at their door.
