# The Interview Process

The opening sections of *Cracking the Coding Interview* demystify the machinery: how questions are chosen, what's being scored, and what happens behind the scenes at the big companies.

> **Note:** This module is a study guide written for Atlas CE, not a copy. Cracking the Coding Interview is a commercial book by Gayle Laakmann McDowell — buy it at [careercup.com](https://www.careercup.com/book). Each topic below distills one idea from the book's first three sections.

## Why This Format Exists

Algorithm and coding problems are the largest component at top companies — often one question fills a ~45-minute interview, and you're expected to talk out loud while solving. Companies accept false negatives (rejecting good candidates) because false positives worry them more; the format also proxies problem-solving skill and basic data-structures knowledge, and the whiteboard strips boilerplate so the discussion is the meat of the problem. Pseudocode alone is not acceptable, though.

If the interviewer jumps in with hints, that's normal and not a failure signal — hints needed is just one of the things being measured.

**The norm:** talk through your reasoning; hints mid-problem are part of the process, not a bad sign.

## What Interviewers Actually Score

Evaluation is across roughly five axes, weighted differently by company and role:

| Axis | What's observed |
| --- | --- |
| Analytical | Optimality, time to solution, how you structure the problem and weigh tradeoffs |
| Coding | Clean, organized code; awareness of errors; good style |
| Technical knowledge | Computer-science fundamentals and stack depth |
| Experience | Past technical decisions, project depth, initiative |
| Culture / communication | How you work with the person across the table |

And the catch candidates forget: **it's all relative.** You're compared against everyone that interviewer has ever given that question to. Getting a hard question is not a bad sign — it was hard for everyone. There are no official company question lists; each interviewer picks their own, so a "Google question" is essentially the same as a Facebook one.

**The norm:** the goal is to do better than other candidates on the same question, not to be flawless.

## The General Flow: Screen, Then On-Site

Almost everywhere: one or two **screening interviews** (phone, often technical, with the bar as high as on-site — code via shared doc or read back your paper code), then an **on-site** of 3–6 in-person interviews, one of which is usually a non-technical lunch (feedback often still happens, or deliberately doesn't). Decisions come from an interviewer meeting, a hiring manager, or a hiring committee.

Silence means nothing — most companies respond in about a week; check in politely after 3–5 business days. Rejection is rarely permanent: re-application windows are typically 6 months to a year, and a prior bad interview barely matters the second time.

**The norm:** treat the phone screen as the real interview; follow up politely, and re-apply after waiting out the window.

## Behind the Scenes: The Big Five

- **Microsoft:** a warm-up from the recruiter (be nice — they advocate for you); 4–5 interviews possibly across two teams, held in interviewers' offices. Reaching the hiring manager means you likely passed that team. They probe passion for technology — prepare "why Microsoft".
- **Amazon:** phone screen with a specific team; 4–5 on-sites where each interviewer owns one area and can't see others' feedback. The **bar raiser** — trained, from another team, with veto power — guards the hiring bar. Scalability and object-oriented design come up.
- **Google:** engineer phone screen at on-site standard; 4–6 on-sites; written feedback scored in four categories (analytical, coding, experience, communication) on a 1–4 scale for a hiring committee that wants at least one *enthusiastic* endorser — 3.6/3.1/3.1/2.6 beats all-3.1s. Offers then pass compensation and executive committees; weeks are normal. Algorithms matter at every seniority.
- **Apple:** recruiter screen, technical phone screens, then 6–8 on-sites mixing one-on-one and two-on-one; the lunch with your potential manager is an interview. Meeting the director and VP is a very good sign. Show passion for the products.
- **Facebook:** 1–2 technical phone screens, sometimes homework (get it code-reviewed — style counts). On-site roles: **Jedi** (behavioral/culture), **Ninja** (coding/algorithms, usually two), **Pirate** (design/architecture, mostly for experienced candidates). Hired "in general", then a six-week bootcamp. Show you love building fast.

**The norm:** learn your target's shape — who decides, what they probe, what passion they're testing for.

## Special Situations

- **Experienced candidates** still get algorithm questions — some interviewers grade easier, some harder, and it averages out. System design and behavioral answers are graded relative to your years and expected to run deeper.
- **SDETs** must prep double: classic "test a pen" problems, full developer-level coding (the top rejection reason), and a "now test it" follow-up on any coding answer.
- **Dev leads and managers** face strong coding bars almost everywhere, plus leadership, prioritization and "getting things done".
- **Startups** hire through referrals most of all, weigh personality fit and exact stack match, and can't always sponsor visas.
- **Acquisitions** interview most or all employees; results decide who gets offers and even the price — teams prep for weeks before a date is set.

**The norm:** prep relative to your lane — the bar moves with the role, not away from coding.
