# Before the Interview

Section IV of *Cracking the Coding Interview*: the work that happens long before the phone screen decides most of the outcome.

> **Note:** This module is a study guide written for Atlas CE, not a copy. Cracking the Coding Interview is a commercial book by Gayle Laakmann McDowell — buy it at [careercup.com](https://www.careercup.com/book). Each topic below distills one idea from the book's fourth section.

## Getting the Right Experience

Companies want proof of two things: you're smart, and you can code. Students get there with big-project courses, early internships, and starting something — a personal project, a hackathon entry, an open-source contribution. Professionals get there by steering toward "meaty" coding work and building on nights and weekends. And plan ahead for the *next* jump: if you'll eventually need leadership experience, start collecting it now.

**The norm:** experience is built on purpose, in advance — not discovered in the interview.

## A Resume That Survives Ten Seconds

Recruiters spend about a decade of seconds per resume; length signals failure to prioritize, not experience. One page in the US until roughly ten years of experience. The strong bullet formula: **"Accomplished X by implementing Y, which led to Z"** — with a measurable Z ("reduced object rendering time by 75% by implementing a distributed cache, cutting login time by 10%").

List your 2–4 most significant projects with what they were and the technologies; independent projects beat course projects because they show initiative. State languages with explicit proficiency — "Java (expert), C++ (proficient)" — since years-used is a weak metric. Know the stigmas: enterprise-only languages, flaunting five flavors of one language, certifications (neutral-to-negative at top companies). Diversify across genuinely different languages, get a native speaker to proofread if you're not one, and omit age, marital status and nationality on US resumes.

**The norm:** every bullet earns its line with X, Y, Z; cut everything else.

## The Preparation Map

The book's timeline, in order:

1. Build projects outside school and work; learn multiple languages; expand your network.
2. Master Big O.
3. Implement the core data structures and algorithms from scratch — lists, trees, graphs, hash tables, sorts.
4. Make a target company list; draft and review the resume.
5. Create the **interview preparation grid** (see the behavioral module).
6. Form a mock-interview group; keep a running list of your mistakes; practice until mocks feel routine.
7. Before each interview: re-read the technical and behavioral strategy, print the powers-of-2 table for phone screens, and practice talking out loud.

Stumbling mid-interview is normal — the habit built beforehand is what carries you through it.

**The norm:** prep is a checklist with a timeline, not a vibe.

## Pick One Language and Know It Cold

For interviews, one primary language beats five shallow ones. Weigh: whether interviewers will know it, how readable its syntax is to a stranger, its potential for problems (pointer and memory bugs in C++ eat interview time), verbosity (verbose languages cost writing time — mitigate with explained abbreviations), and ease of use (multiple return values, rich libraries — or just assume helper functions exist). The strongest language you already own usually wins; whatever you pick, know its collections, its string handling and its standard library cold.

**The norm:** interview in your best language, prepared deep — not the trendiest one.
